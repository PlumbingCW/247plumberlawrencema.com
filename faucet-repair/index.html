Faucet repair in Lawrence, MA means fixing the part that controls water at a sink, tub, shower or outdoor spigot. A drip is a nuisance. A broken handle, a split supply line or a valve that will not shut is an emergency, and our line is open 24/7 for it.

While you wait, find the small shutoff valve under the sink or behind the fixture and turn it clockwise. If it will not move, close the main water valve for the unit and open the lowest tap to drain the line.

If water is spraying right now

Do these in order. Each one takes under a minute.

1

Find the local stop

Look under the sink for two oval or round handles. Turn each clockwise until it stops. Tubs and showers rarely have one, so skip ahead.

2

Close the main

Shut the unit's main water valve if the local stop fails. In a multifamily building, ask neighbors before you shut a shared riser.

3

Kill the fill

Open a lower faucet or the outdoor spigot to drain pressure from the pipe. Do not force a stuck valve with a wrench.

4

Dry the area

Move food, rugs and electronics out of the splash zone, and turn off power to wet outlets only if you can reach the breaker without standing in water.

A broken or burst supply valve

The small valve under the sink is called a supply stop, and it is often the weak point. Old stops seize, crack at the stem or weep at the packing nut. If the handle snaps off in your hand, water may come out around the stem and you cannot shut it by hand.

In that case the fix starts at the main. A plumber closes the supply upstream, replaces the stop and the supply line, and tests it under full pressure. Replacing a stop that fails every few years is cheaper than repeating a flood.

Corroded or seized stops are common in older buildings. Lawrence's Housing Production Plan puts about 83 percent of units before 1979, so ask for a quarter-turn ball stop rather than another multi-turn type. Replacement fixtures and supply valves are installed to the Massachusetts plumbing code (248 CMR 10.00) and to the fixture manufacturer's standards.

Which faucet problem do you have

The symptom usually points to the failed part.

Drip from the spout

Often a worn cartridge, washer or seat. Repair is usually fast if the fixture is still made.

Water at the handle base

Typically an O-ring or packing nut. A stripped handle screw can hide the real source.

Low or uneven flow

May be a clogged aerator or a partly closed stop. Mineral buildup can also be inside the body.

Hot and cold mix

Often a bad cartridge or check stop. A single-handle fixture can lose its seal over time.

Leak under the sink

Could be the supply line, the drain connection or the faucet shank. A paper towel test helps find it.

Handle spins loosely

The set screw or cartridge stem is stripped. Replacement is more likely than repair.

Shower valve and showerhead leaks

A showerhead that drips after you turn it off is usually a worn cartridge or valve seat. A fixture that leaks inside the wall is different. A stain on the ceiling below, a soft spot in the wall or a musty smell can mean a leaking valve body, which requires access.

When the valve is behind tile, the plumber opens the wall from the back if it can be reached, or removes the trim and cartridge first. Ask for the cause in writing before tile is cut, and for leak detection if the source is unclear.

Tub faucets and diverters

A tub spout that sends water to the showerhead even when set to tub often has a worn diverter. A tub that drips from the spout after a bath is a cartridge job. Both are quick when parts are in stock and slow when the fixture has been discontinued.

Repair the faucet or replace it

Each has a place. The call depends on age, finish and parts.

Repair it when

  • The fixture is fairly recent and parts are available.
  • Only the cartridge, seat or O-ring has failed.
  • You like the finish and the body is not pitted.
  • A repair keeps the supply and mounting intact.

Replace it when

  • The body is corroded, cracked or pitted.
  • Parts are discontinued or cost near the price of a new unit.
  • Handles feel loose after two repairs.
  • You want a different style, flow rate or sprayer.

Replacing a faucet is plumbing work in a home, so it falls under the licensed-plumber and permit rules. Ask the local plumbing inspector how your job is classified.

How a plumber finds the failing part

The plumber tests before removing parts.

1

Confirm the source

Dry the area, run the fixture and watch for the first bead of water to find the true leak path.

2

Check pressure and stops

A gauge on the hose bib shows house pressure. High pressure can wear out cartridges early.

3

Isolate the supply

Each hot and cold stop is closed and tested, to see which side is feeding the leak.

4

Disassemble and inspect

Cartridge, seat, O-rings and the body are checked, then you decide on repair or replace.

Outdoor spigots and winter freeze

A hose spigot that freezes can split inside the wall and leak only when you open it in spring. State code lists a frost depth of 48 inches, and NOAA's January average low for Lawrence is about 19.6 F, so exposed spigots and attic lines are at risk.

The city's rules say it assumes no responsibility for frozen service lines, and its guidance is to keep the thermostat at 55 F or higher and call a plumber. Shut off and drain a hose bib before freezing weather, and call us if one has already split, since a burst pipe repair may be needed.
