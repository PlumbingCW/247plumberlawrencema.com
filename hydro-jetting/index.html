Hydro jetting is a drain cleaning method that sends water through a hose and nozzle at very high pressure to scrub the inside of a pipe. It is different from a cable, which pokes a hole through a clog. Jetting is not right for every pipe, and we say so before starting.

Slow drains that keep coming back after every snaking

You had the line snaked. It ran free for a few weeks, then slowed again, then backed up. That pattern usually means the cable opened a channel but left a coating of grease, soap, scale or silt on the pipe wall, which then catches the next load.

The fix for that pattern has a technical name: hydro jetting. The nozzle sends jets backward to scour the wall and forward to pull itself through the line, so the whole pipe gets cleaned, not just the plug.

A drain that clogs once a year is a different story. Jetting earns its cost when the same line has failed two or three times, or when a kitchen, laundry or main drain handles heavy use.

When a backed-up line is an emergency

Call right away if sewage or gray water is coming up through a floor drain, tub or toilet, or if several fixtures stop draining at once. A repeat clog that has finally closed the line completely is usually that kind of case.

After hours, we start with the fastest safe step, which is often a cable to restore flow. A camera and a jetting visit can follow once the water is moving and the pipe can be checked. Our sewer line cleaning page explains the difference between a branch drain and the main line.

What hydro jetting is, and what it is not

Plumbers use the word loosely, and so do advertisements. Here is the plain version.

Hydro jetting is

  • A pressurized water hose with a multi-port nozzle
  • A way to wash grease, soap, sludge and loose scale off the pipe wall
  • A good match for repeat clogs and for long runs of drain
  • Often followed by a camera look to confirm the result

Hydro jetting is not

  • A repair for a cracked, offset or collapsed pipe
  • A cure for roots that have grown through a joint (roots return)
  • A safe choice for brittle or heavily corroded pipe
  • A substitute for fixing a sag that holds standing water

The tool matters less than the match between the tool and the pipe. If a plumber wants to jet without ever looking inside the line, ask why.

When a camera comes first

Pressure that cleans a sound pipe can finish off a weak one. These are the situations where we look inside before jetting.

  • The building is older and the drain material is unknown or mixed.
  • The cable has pulled out rust flakes, clay chips or pieces of old pipe.
  • The clog returned within days, not months, which suggests a belly or break.
  • Water pools in the line after it drains, which can mean a sag.
  • The house had a recent repair, so a patch or coupling may be weaker than the pipe around it.
  • The line serves several apartments and a failure would flood a lower unit.
  • You are buying the house and want to know what condition the line is in.

The footage can show scale, roots, offsets and cracks. The sewer camera inspection page covers what each one looks like.

While you wait for the plumber

A few quick moves protect your floor and speed up the job.

1

Stop all water

No dishwasher, no laundry, no showers. A blocked line fills quickly, and the water has to go somewhere.

2

Protect the floor

Move boxes and electronics away from the floor drain and wet areas. Shut off power to a flooded outlet area at the panel only if it is dry and safe to reach.

3

Locate the cleanout

A capped pipe near the basement floor or outside wall is the usual entry. Clear a path, but do not open it if water is rising.

4

Note the history

Write down when the drain last clogged, how it was cleared, and any past camera work. It helps the plumber choose a method.

How a jetting visit runs

The sequence is straightforward. The hardest part is deciding whether to start at all.

1

Assess the pipe

We look at the cleanout, the symptoms and any camera footage to decide if jetting suits the pipe.

2

Set up

We position a hose through the cleanout, protect the surrounding floor and use a collection point so water does not spill.

3

Jet the line

The nozzle works forward and back through the line, flushing debris to the main or a catch point.

4

Verify

We run water, and often a camera, to confirm the pipe is clear and to note anything that needs repair.
