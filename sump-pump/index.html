Water on the basement floor and the pump is silent, or never stops

Most calls start the same way. The floor is wet, and the pump hums without moving water, sits dead, or runs nonstop without catching up. Each points to a different fault.

A humming pump often has a jammed impeller or a failed check valve. A silent one may have lost power or burned out. One that never shuts off can be undersized, stuck on its float, or pushing against a blocked or frozen discharge line. The technical name for the fix is sump pump repair or replacement.

When basement water is an emergency

Treat it as urgent when water is rising, when it nears the furnace, water heater or electrical panel, or when the pump quit during heavy rain or fast snowmelt. Ask for a same-day or after-hours visit in those cases, because standing water soaks drywall and framing deeper by the hour.

Sewage smell changes things. That points to an ejector or sewer backup, not groundwater, so keep children and pets out of the room and call the line.

While you wait for the plumber

A few safe steps limit the damage.

1

Stay clear of electricity

If water is near outlets or appliances, do not wade in. Leave the breaker alone unless you can reach it from a dry spot.

2

Lift what you can

Move boxes and furniture off the floor. Wet cardboard and drywall fail fast.

3

Check the plug

See whether a breaker or GFCI outlet tripped. Reset once. Repeated trips mean stop and call.

4

Note what you see

Write down whether the pump hums, clicks or stays silent, and where water enters.

Signs the pump is failing before the basement floods

Pumps often warn first.

  • The motor runs long after the pit looks empty, which can mean a stuck float or a failed check valve.
  • Grinding, rattling or a high whine that was not there last spring.
  • The pump switches on and off every few seconds, which wears the switch quickly.
  • Rust, silt or debris in the pit, or a float rubbing the wall.

How we find out why the water is winning

We work from the pit outward so the real cause gets fixed.

1

Look at the pit

We check its size, the lid, debris and whether the float moves freely.

2

Test the pump

We fill the pit, watch the float and switch, and confirm water actually moves. We also check power and GFCI protection.

3

Trace the discharge

We follow the pipe to where it ends. Kinks, clogs, frozen sections and a missing check valve are common culprits.

4

Find the water source

Sometimes a leaking supply line or foundation crack adds water. We tell you which part is plumbing.

Repair the pump or replace it

The choice depends on age and what failed.

Repair makes sense when

  • The pump is fairly new and the fault is a switch, float or check valve.
  • The pit and discharge line are sound.
  • The pump size matches the water it has to move.

Replace makes sense when

  • The motor is burned out or the housing is cracked.
  • It has been rebuilt more than once.
  • It short cycles because it is oversized for the pit.

We give the clear price before work starts, for either choice.

Battery backup, because storms take the power too

A primary pump only works when the house has power. Heavy rain and high winds often arrive together, which is when a basement needs the pump most. A battery backup pump takes over when the main one fails or power drops.

Battery units need periodic testing and an eventual battery swap. Water-powered backups depend on municipal water pressure, so ask before assuming one fits.

Check valve and discharge, where code and common sense meet

Massachusetts plumbing code, 248 CMR 10.15, requires an accessible check valve on sewage ejector and sump discharge piping. It keeps water from falling back into the pit, so the pump does not restart over and over.

Plumbing work that is replaced or repaired needs a permit from the local inspector, so ask the local plumbing inspector whether a pump swap or discharge change counts. Sump pump or sewage ejector

A sump pump moves groundwater out of a pit. A sewage ejector lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the sewer line, with a sealed lid and vent. A backed-up ejector may also need a look at the sewer line.
