Arlington is one of the best known parts of Lawrence, and it sits mostly in the 01841 ZIP code. If you live or own property here, your plumbing problem is usually shaped by two things: the age and layout of the building, and the low ground that has taken water before.

We run a call-only line, so there is no form to fill out. Read the sections below if you want to know what to check first, then call and describe what you see.

What the city's own plan says about Arlington

Lawrence's Housing Production Plan for 2018 to 2022 states that the Arlington neighborhood flooded significantly in 2001 and again in 2006. After the repeated floods, a part of the neighborhood near Marion Avenue was designated a FEMA flood zone in 2001. That area is now Kennedy Playstead, a city park.

For an owner or a landlord the practical point is simple. A basement that has taken water before needs a plan that starts with where the water goes, not with paint or patching. The same plan says more than 17 percent of Lawrence lies in the 100 and 500 year floodplains, so flood history is a citywide matter and not something unique to one street.

When the basement takes water: start with the path

A sump pump moves water from a pit in the basement floor to a discharge pipe outside. In Lawrence that pipe should not tie into the sanitary sewer. The Greater Lawrence Sanitary District, which treats the city's sewage, tells homeowners to keep sump pumps and basement drains out of the sanitary system.

The discharge line also needs a check valve that you can reach, as the state plumbing code requires (248 CMR 10.15), so water does not run back into the pit when the pump stops. If the pump runs but the pit keeps filling, the pipe, the valve or the outlet is the first place to look. Our sump pump service page covers the parts, and basement water control explains when drainage work is a better answer than a pump.

One money point before a storm, not after. The Massachusetts Division of Insurance says standard homeowner policies usually exclude sewer backup and sump pump failure unless an endorsement was added. Ask your agent what you have.

One building, several households

Arlington has many buildings with more than one unit. Who does what depends on which side of the lease you are on.

If you own the building

  • The drain stack and main shutoff serve every unit.
  • If more than one unit backs up, ask for a camera look at the shared line.
  • Replacement or repair work usually needs a plumbing permit from the city.
  • Keep the shutoff locations written on the basement wall.

If you rent a unit

  • Report the problem to your landlord in writing and keep a copy.
  • The state sanitary code (105 CMR 410) sets hot water at 110 to 130 F.
  • A full day or more without enough water is treated as endangering health and safety.
  • Take dated photos before anyone repairs or cleans up.

This is general information and not legal advice. Housing code questions go to Lawrence Inspectional Services at 978-620-3130.

Shared drains and mill-era blocks

When two apartments in one house gurgle or back up at the same time, the blockage is often downstream of both, in the stack or the lateral. The lateral is the pipe from the building to the city sewer main, and in Lawrence the property owner owns it. A sewer camera inspection shows whether the cause is grease, roots or a broken section before anyone digs.

Routine blockages are a job for drain cleaning. The big brick mill buildings around Arlington Mills are a different animal, with long runs and several tenants, and they fall under commercial plumbing. If you are not sure which pipe is yours, confirm with Lawrence Water and Sewer at 978-620-3110.

Before you call about an Arlington plumbing problem

A minute of notes saves a long phone call.

1

Find your shutoff

Locate the main valve near the meter and any valve under the fixture. Turn it clockwise to close.

2

Count the affected units

Note whether one fixture, one apartment or the whole building is involved.

3

Check the sump pump

Look for a tripped plug, a stuck float or a pit that is full and silent.

4

Photograph the problem

Pictures of the pipe, the label on the water heater and any water line help us ask better questions.
