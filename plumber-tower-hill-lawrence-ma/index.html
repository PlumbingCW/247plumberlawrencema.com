Plumber in Tower Hill, Lawrence, MA

Tower Hill is the highest ground in Lawrence and the home of the city's historic water tower and reservoir. This page covers water lines, meters and the questions dense buildings raise.

Tower Hill has its own neighborhood association, which meets on the last Wednesday of most months at Judson Hall, 526 Lowell Street. The city housing plan calls the water tower and reservoir on the hill among Lawrence's most important historic sites, and says the reservoir is still the city's main water storage.

A 2025 Merrimack Valley Planning Commission study of Lower Tower Hill noted high residential density, with many parcels at 17 or more dwelling units per acre. Dense buildings mean shared pipes, which is why the questions below matter.

The hill, the tower and what they tell you

The Tower Hill standpipe dates to 1896, and the city's water department lists the Tower Hill tank among the places it stores finished water. A local history source puts the hill near 240 feet above sea level, the highest point in Lawrence.

Water pressure in a building depends on many things, including height above storage, pipe size and a pressure reducing valve. We make no claim about pressure on Tower Hill. If pressure drops suddenly, check your own main valve and pressure reducing valve first, then ask Lawrence Water and Sewer about the street.

Lead service lines: what the city has published

The city's most recent water quality report lists lead at 0.000 mg/L at the 90th percentile, against an action level of 0.015, from 30 sampled sites. Lawrence reports 12,777 service lines in total, with 126 lead lines replaced from 2017 onward, up to 57 lead lines that may remain and 345 of unknown material.

Since January 2014 new lead cannot be introduced into a water service, so a lead line cannot be repaired. It can only be replaced. The city has a public service line inventory map, so look your address up first. If the map shows lead or unknown, ask the city what it has on record, then talk to a plumber about water line repair and replacement.

Who owns which pipe, and what frozen lines mean

Lawrence water division rules say the city repairs from the main to the curb stop and the owner is responsible from the curb stop onward. A city web page says the owner is responsible up to and including the meter, so confirm with Lawrence Water and Sewer at 978-620-3110 before you assume.

The same rules say the city assumes no responsibility for frozen service lines. The Massachusetts building code (780 CMR) puts frost depth at 48 inches, and the January average low in Lawrence is 19.6 F. The city advises keeping the thermostat at 55 F or higher and insulating exposed pipes. If a pipe has already split, see burst pipe repair. For a hidden leak behind a wall or under a slab, leak detection finds it with less demolition.

Signs the water line needs attention

None of these proves a problem, but each is a reason to look.

  • Low pressure at every fixture at once, not just one tap.
  • A wet patch or soft ground near the meter or the basement wall where the line enters.
  • A water bill that rises with no change in how you live.
  • The sound of running water when every fixture is off.
  • Brown or cloudy water that clears slowly after the tap runs.

Older pipe inside the walls

Inside a building, supply pipe fails for different reasons than the line outside. Fittings corrode, old valves seize and a hot water heater can leak at its base. When leaks keep showing up in more than one place, repiping becomes a fair question. For a leaking tank, see water heater repair to learn what can be fixed and what cannot.
