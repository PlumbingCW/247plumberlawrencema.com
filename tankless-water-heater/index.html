A tankless water heater in Lawrence, MA heats water as it flows instead of storing it, so the supply does not run out until demand outruns the burner. It sits on a wall, draws gas or electricity only while a tap is open, and takes far less floor space than a 40 or 50 gallon tank.

The catch is that a tankless unit asks more of your gas line, venting and water than a tank does. This page explains how to decide, how to maintain one and when to call.

Running out of hot water halfway through a shower?

A shower that goes cold after ten minutes or a house where the dishwasher steals heat from the bath is a classic sign of an undersized or tired storage tank. The technical answer is a tankless water heater, which heats on demand instead of draining a fixed supply.

Not every cold shower needs a tankless unit. A failed element, a bad dip tube or sediment can cause the same symptom in a standard tank, and a repair may be cheaper. A plumber should rule those out before selling a new system, and our page on water heater repair covers the usual faults.

Flushing and descaling a tankless heater

Tankless units have narrow heat exchanger passages, and minerals in the water can coat them with scale. Scale makes the unit work harder, trigger error codes, shorten bursts of heat and reduce efficiency. Flushing with a mild descaling solution, circulated through the unit by a pump, dissolves that buildup.

How often depends on your water. The annual water quality report from Lawrence Water & Sewer does not list hardness, so test your tap before choosing an interval. Harder water often calls for yearly flushing, and softer water may allow longer gaps. The manufacturer manual sets a baseline.

What a flush involves

The plumber isolates the unit using service valves, connects a pump and hoses, and circulates the solution for a period. The inlet screen is cleaned. Everything is flushed with clean water and the unit is tested for temperature and flow.

Signs it needs descaling now

Error codes that mention flow or temperature, water that swings from hot to lukewarm, or a unit that clicks and cycles can all point to scale. Without service valves on the unit, a flush is awkward, so ask about adding them.

Gas line and venting: what a tankless unit demands

A gas tankless heater can burn several times the fuel of a tank heater at peak. If the existing pipe is sized for a small tank, it may starve the burner and cause the unit to shut down under load. Massachusetts requires gas piping to be sized under NFPA 54 as adopted in the state, and a licensed gas fitter must do the work under a gas permit.

Venting is just as important. Most tankless gas units use a sealed, direct-vent system with plastic or stainless pipe, and condensing models drain acidic condensate that needs a neutralizer and a drain. Old masonry chimneys are not usually suitable without a liner.

If you smell gas near any appliance, leave the building, call 911 from outside, then call Eversource Gas at (800) 525-8222. Do not call a plumber first.

Where tankless fits and where it struggles

The match between the unit and the building matters more than the brand.

Small households

One or two people with steady use often get the clearest benefit from on-demand heat and a freed-up closet.

Large families

Several showers at once can exceed the flow of a single unit. Plumbers sometimes pair two units or step up to a bigger one.

Cold winter water

Incoming water is often much colder in a New England winter, which lowers the flow a unit can heat to temperature.

Multi-unit buildings

Separate tankless units per apartment can end shared-tank disputes, though each needs its own gas and vent run.

A load calculation counts fixtures and flow rates and gives a firm answer. Ask for one before you sign anything, and see water heater installation for how the visit and permit work.

Tankless in older Lawrence buildings

About 53 percent of Lawrence housing was built in 1939 or earlier, and roughly 12 percent of units sit in two-family buildings. Older buildings often have small gas lines, tight basements and shared flues, which is why a tankless conversion should begin with a site visit, not a phone quote.
