Plumber in North Common, Lawrence, MA

North Common is the historic area around Campagnone Common, with older multi-unit buildings and some shops. This page is about shared drains, old layouts and who handles what.

The Lawrence Housing Production Plan names the North Common neighborhood among the areas where historic districts overlap. The 17.5 acre park at its center was donated by the Essex Company and was part of the city's original plan. It is now Campagnone North Common.

Data sites place Canal, Union, Common, Essex and Newbury Streets in or near the area and show a high share of three- and four-unit buildings. That layout drives most of the plumbing questions below.

A planned neighborhood with old rules

The city plan says the houses and buildings facing the Common once followed specific rules on height, material and use. Methuen Street and Union Street front the nearby North Canal Historic District, and Essex Street is the historic main street. The result is a neighborhood of older buildings that were built, altered and divided many times.

A building renovated in pieces over many years can hold several pipe materials and several generations of repairs. We do not claim what any building here contains. We do say that a shared layout makes it worth looking before you pay for a repair.

Apartments upstairs, a shop downstairs

Mixed-use blocks put different plumbing demands on one drain.

Residential side

  • Kitchen and bath drains join a shared stack.
  • Laundry loads can overwhelm a small drain.
  • Several units may share one water heater or one meter.
  • Tenants should report problems to the owner in writing.

Shop or restaurant side

  • Grease and food waste can block a shared line.
  • Floor drains may connect to the same stack.
  • Backflow devices on some connections need testing by a certified tester.
  • See commercial plumbing for what that involves.

Massachusetts requires backflow testers to be certified by MassDEP, and double check assemblies are tested every year (310 CMR 22.22).

Old drains, a camera and when jetting makes sense

Routine slow drains call for drain cleaning, and a cable usually clears them. When a line backs up again within weeks, a camera look is the next step. It shows what is actually clogging or damaging the line.

High-pressure water cleaning, called hydro jetting, scours a pipe wall that a cable only pokes through. It suits a grease-lined or scaled line, but only if the pipe can take it, which is what the camera tells you. Larger blockages in the pipe that leaves the building are a job for sewer line cleaning.

Questions for the owner of a multi-unit building here

Answer these before the next problem, not during it.

  • Where is the main water shutoff, and does each unit have its own valve?
  • Do several units share one water heater or one drain stack?
  • When was the sewer lateral last inspected with a camera?
  • Who holds the keys to the basement and the meter room?
  • Does any exterior change need approval because of the historic district?

North Common plumbing questions

The city plan treats it as a neighborhood around a public park. Streets near it hold apartments, houses and shops, so check how your own building is used before choosing a service.

Stacks are shared, so grease or a blockage from one use can show up in another. A camera look finds where the problem starts.

Clearing a clog is one of the few jobs Massachusetts plumbing rules treat as permit-free. Forcing a cable through an old pipe can still damage it, so look with a camera if the clog keeps coming back.

The city maintains the main and the owner maintains the lateral. Report sewer overflows to the city at 978-620-3580 or 978-620-3110.

The city plan places part of the neighborhood in historic districts. We cannot say how those rules apply to a given job, so ask Inspectional Services at 978-620-3130 before changing anything visible from the street.
